Somethin’ Fishy · an interactive field guide

What actually changed in the boats’ movement?

Start with a boat pulled in several directions. Turn those pulls into one effective target. Then see exactly where the repaired solver changes the mathematics.

There are three layers: an exact change of coordinates, a smooth arrival rule, and feedback that recalculates the attractions. They solve different problems. The first preserves the instantaneous heading; the other two change the controller formulation.

01 / The original model

A compass, with a separate throttle

Each fishing ground contributes an arrow pointing from the boat toward that ground. The arrow is stronger when its attraction control is larger, its fish stock is larger, or its distance penalty is smaller.

Aj = uj gj Fj dj
s = ∑j Aj
ẋ = v · s / ‖s‖
What do the symbols mean?

x is the boat’s position; fj is ground j’s position. Fj is its remaining fish. The control uj changes that ground’s attraction, while v controls the boat’s physical speed.

ρj = ‖fj − x‖   distancedj = (fj − x) / ρj   unit directiongj = exp(−αρj²)   distance weight

The illustrations focus on one boat, so the boat index i is omitted. The subscript j still labels fishing grounds.

The final division removes the length of the combined arrow. Its direction tells the boat where to go; v separately sets the speed. These arrows are steering signals, not forces in an acceleration equation. There is no inertia, turning radius, or momentum in this model.

Experiment: change direction without changing speed

Increase one attraction. Then halve both together. The arrow lengths change in both experiments, but only the relative balance changes the heading.

Predict first: if every attraction becomes ten times weaker, does the boat slow down?

No, provided the combined arrow remains nonzero. Multiplying every attraction by the same positive constant multiplies both s and ‖s‖ by that constant, so it cancels. The speed remains v. The old code’s tiny denominator floors could violate this property; removing their unintended throttling restores the written movement law.

02 / Describe the same direction differently

The effective target is a weighted average of grounds

Exact algebra, where defined

Several arrows can point a boat toward a place that is not itself a fishing ground. If one ground is ahead-left and another ahead-right, their combined influence can point between them. We represent that direction using one effective target z.

The same controls, viewed as one target

This diagram uses the controls above. The target slides along the line between the two grounds. With more grounds, it lies inside their convex hull.

The correct weights are not just uj. They also account for stock, distance, and the normalization of each ground’s direction. Define ρj as the boat-to-ground distance:

bj = uj gj Fj / ρjB = ∑j bj,   θj = bj / Bz = ∑j θj fj
Follow the algebra, one line at a time
s = ∑j bj(fj − x)= ∑j bjfj − Bx= Bz − Bx = B(z − x)s / ‖s‖ = (z − x) / ‖z − x‖

B is positive when at least one attraction is positive and stocks are positive. It disappears when we normalize. This identity requires nonzero net direction and nonzero boat-to-ground distances.

Because the θ weights are nonnegative and sum to one, z lies in the convex hull: the region a rubber band would enclose around all the fishing grounds. With two grounds, that region is their connecting line. This constrains steering; z is not an arbitrary point anywhere on the map.

The optimizer now chooses z rather than searching through many attraction magnitudes that all encode the same direction. This coordinate change alone does not require a new movement law. The equivalent unsmoothed law is ẋ = v(z − x)/‖z − x‖.

A target’s position is not a compulsory waypoint. It determines the instantaneous direction. The optimizer can change the target later, and the boat collects fish according to where it physically goes—not according to which target it selects.

03 / Why normalization can be fragile

A tiny imbalance can reverse a full-speed heading

Place one ground directly left and another directly right. If their effective pulls are almost equal, the sum is tiny. A slight imbalance decides whether the boat moves left or right at its full commanded speed.

Move through the cancellation point

Grounds are at −1 and +1; boat is at 0. Effective pull strengths are 1 and 1 + imbalance. V = a = v = 1, η = 0.5. This compares the same effective target under the two movement laws.

At exact cancellation, the original heading contains 0/0. This is different from multiplying all attractions by a tiny common factor: common scaling leaves a well-defined heading intact; cancellation destroys the directional signal.

04 / The deliberate change to movement

Approach the effective target smoothly

Genuine regularization

Before

ẋ = v (z − x) / ‖z − x‖

For a fixed nonzero v, distance does not reduce speed. The direction is undefined at z = x.

After

ẋ = aV (z − x) / √(‖z − x‖² + η²)

a is a commanded fraction between 0 and 1. The denominator is never zero because η is positive.

If d is the distance to the effective target, the actual speed is:

vactual = aV · d / √(d² + η²)

Far from the target, the factor is almost one. Close to it, the factor falls toward zero. For the project’s η = 0.5, it is about 0.995 at distance 5 and 0.707 at distance 0.5. The after-law remains within the original speed bound.

Arrival experiment: a fixed target

Both boats begin 2 units from the target, with full commanded throttle and V = 1. The original curve is defined only until contact; the illustration does not invent a stopping rule afterward.

This is slowing near z, not slowing near every fishing ground. A boat can pass close to a ground at high speed when its effective target lies elsewhere. It can also stop anywhere by setting a = 0. For a fixed target and positive throttle, the regularized motion approaches that target asymptotically.

Could the original controller already slow down?

Yes. Its speed control v could be reduced manually or by optimization. The new family explicitly ties speed to target distance through v = aVd/√(d² + η²). Thus every regularized velocity can be realized using an admissible original speed, but for a fixed target the new family excludes some higher original speeds. Choosing a farther target along the same ray can reduce that restriction when the hull permits it. We do not claim the full optimization problem is unchanged.

Why not simply choose η = 0?

That restores the unsmoothed expression away from the target, but also restores its undefined value at the target and its severe directional sensitivity near cancellation. η is a mathematical regularization, not just an integrator tolerance. The project value is 0.5; changing this slider is an illustration, not a change to the saved project policy.

05 / The execution change

Maintain the target by recalculating attractions

State-feedback execution

Suppose two grounds originally contribute balanced pulls. If the fish at the left ground decrease while the raw attractions stay fixed, its pull weakens and the heading bends toward the right ground.

The new controller recalculates the attraction values using the current positions and stocks, so they still produce the chosen effective target. It preserves the intended steering while the state changes. The target schedule was optimized with fish depletion in mind; it is not automatically reoptimized at every instant.

Same target, different fish stock

At the reference state, both controls are 10 and both grounds are full. The chosen target is their midpoint. Change the left stock or the boat’s position to compare fixed raw controls with reconstructed controls.

Fixed raw controlsReconstructed feedback controls
uj = c · θjρj / (gjFj)
Choose the common positive scale c so all u values stay at or below 10.

If a ground’s fish stock halves, its relative attraction may need to double to maintain the same target. That does not require exceeding the bound: we can scale every attraction down together without changing direction. The example starts with (10, 10); at half the left stock and zero position offset, feedback uses (10, 5).

The production implementation selects a well-conditioned pair of ground directions and uses logarithmic scaling. The formula above explains the inverse construction; it need not reproduce the same individual attraction values as the pair-based implementation. The heading and speed are what must agree.

The equivalence assumes positive fish stocks and nonzero distance to ground centers. Continuous depletion preserves positive stock for finite time, but floating-point underflow still requires care. All tested project paths avoid ground-center singularities.

Does feedback mean we secretly removed fish from the model?

No. Fish still deplete according to the original law and still determine the terminal penalty. Fish also enter the reconstruction of raw attractions. What changes is the controller’s response: it compensates for their effect on steering when maintaining a chosen target. In the transformed equations, motion is independent of fish when z and a are held fixed. That is why the transformed costate equations differ from those for fixed raw attractions.

07 / Keep the claims separate

What is equivalent—and what really changed?

ChangeWhat it means
Attractions → effective targetExact instantaneous heading equivalence where defined. Removes redundant magnitudes and exposes the geometry.
Stable normalizationRestores the paper’s direction law when the old code’s denominator floors accidentally reduced speed.
η = 0.5A genuine restriction and regularization of the controller family. Resolves zero effective-target velocity smoothly.
Feedback reconstructionRaw attractions respond to the current state. A frozen raw-attraction table is not the executable final controller.
Fish law, speed and attraction boundsPreserved in physical replay. No inertia, fish regeneration, movement bonus, or mandatory-visit term was added.
Travel-cost smoothingA separate objective change, not a movement law: γv becomes γ(√(v² + ν²) − ν), with ν = 0.001. Its total cost difference is bounded by 0.02 at T = 400; reported comparisons use the original cost.

Successful replay means the resulting feedback trajectories can be executed through the original physical equations. Passing the regularized PMP checks does not prove convergence of the unmodified fixed-raw-control problem or global optimality.

Check your understanding: can z sit where there are no fish?

Yes. z is a steering target, not a claim that fish exist there. A weighted average of grounds often lies between them. Actual harvest depends on the boat’s distances to the real fishing grounds.

Check your understanding: if the boat passes near a ground at full speed, did the regularization fail?

No. Regularization responds to distance from the effective target, not distance from every ground. A distant target can keep the speed factor near one while the boat passes close to another ground.
